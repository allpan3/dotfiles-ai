# Technical writing

Make the intended reader's question and the reasoning needed to answer it determine the document.
Follow the user's requested format, scope, and existing document conventions. Use the relevant
[worked examples](references/examples.md) to calibrate structure before drafting or revising.

Use the workflow below for substantial new work. For revisions, start at the stage the change
requires and preserve unaffected content. Keep working notes brief and out of the deliverable
unless requested. Complete ordinary editorial decisions yourself; involve the user when a missing
detail or substantive choice requires their input.

## 1. Establish the purpose and evidence

Identify the reader, their prior knowledge, the question or task, and the main answer or learning
goal. Establish how the text will be used, such as a formal paper, a self-contained explanation,
or a spoken presentation. Match its register and explanatory detail to that use; choose sectioning
and heading forms separately. Infer these from context; ask only when a missing detail would
materially change the work.
Check that the available evidence supports the answer. Research essential gaps within the task's
scope, or make the uncertainty explicit.

Before planning the document, settle a brief statement of its purpose, the supported answer or
learning goal, and any material limits. For a revision, derive these from the existing document
and the requested change. Proceed when you can explain what the reader should understand,
evaluate, or do, and which evidence the document can rely on.

## 2. Plan the explanation

Plan the section or paragraph order and note what each part contributes. Introduce an essential
concept before relying on it, without overwhelming the opening with definitions. Include material
needed to understand, evaluate, or use the answer. Put optional detail where readers can consult
it without interrupting the explanation.

- For a report or recommendation, make the finding and its material limits clear near the start.
  For a tutorial, establish the goal and guide the reader through the task. Use reference sections
  for lookup. A document can combine these purposes when that serves its readers.
- Choose headings for the document's genre and hierarchy. Major sections in papers and technical
  documents usually name a subject or function, such as "Performance analysis" or "Evaluation".
  Put findings in the text, an optional subtitle, or an appropriate narrower subheading. Task or
  question headings can suit procedures and explanatory material. Use conclusion-style titles
  when they fit the purpose and presentation style, rather than as the default.

Read the planned sequence as a reader: does each part have a distinct purpose, and does the order
provide what the next part needs? Merge overlapping parts, move prerequisites, and remove material
that does not serve the document's purpose before drafting. Reuse an existing structure when it
already serves the reader.

## 3. Draft and revise the structure

Draft from the plan. Connect facts through their actual relationships, such as cause, consequence,
comparison, or evidence. Keep a condition with the claim it qualifies. Use concrete examples to
make an unfamiliar idea understandable. Use lists for parallel items and prose when it makes the
connections between ideas easier to follow.

Review the actual draft for structure before editing individual sentences:

1. Compare the opening with the final conclusions. It should deliver the main answer or establish
   the specific question the explanation resolves. Give a material blocker appropriate prominence.
2. Read the headings in sequence, then summarize each section's and paragraph's contribution in
   a short phrase. Use this to find repeated points, unexplained jumps, missing prerequisites, and
   material that does not serve the purpose. Move, cut, or add content to repair those problems.
3. Check that the evidence supports the claims and that shortening has preserved necessary
   reasoning and qualifications. A missing connection may require more explanation.

Re-read the affected passages after those changes. Complete this pass when the opening matches
the document's purpose, each part makes a needed contribution, and the explanation supplies the
connections and prerequisites the intended reader needs.

## 4. Edit and verify the delivery

Read through the revised text for continuity. Vary sentence length to keep related ideas
connected and emphasis clear. Use `unslop` for sentence-level cleanup when available; keep its
pattern catalog in that skill.

Verify claims against the source material. Preserve source meaning, units, conditions, and
uncertainty. Keep observations distinct from interpretations and recommendations. Distinguish
proposed, implemented, tested, and deployed states when that affects the conclusion. Use only
supported facts and measurements.

For presented slides, make the topic or question clear and give the audience a takeaway supported
by evidence. Use topic, question, or message titles as appropriate to the deck. Review the slide
sequence to check that the audience can follow the explanation. A deck read independently needs
enough explanation to replace what a speaker would say. Select the evidence and context that fit
each slide; move supporting detail to notes or an appendix when needed. Qualify claims wherever
they appear.

Use available document, presentation, PDF, or LaTeX skills for format-specific production. Inspect
the rendered artifact when layout matters, and fix unreadable figures, crowded slides, awkward
page breaks, or headings separated from their content. Before delivery, check that the final
artifact communicates the intended message, supports its claims, and preserves material limits.
