# Technical writing

Make the intended reader's question and the reasoning needed to answer it determine the document.
Follow the user's requested format, scope, and existing document conventions. Use the relevant
[worked examples](references/examples.md) to calibrate the explanation and language before drafting
or revising.

Use the workflow below for substantial new work. For revisions, start at the stage the change
requires and preserve material outside the requested change. Within a passage assigned for language
revision, preserve technical meaning rather than its wording or sentence structure. Keep working
notes brief and out of the deliverable unless requested. Complete ordinary editorial decisions yourself; involve the user when a missing
detail or substantive choice requires their input.

## 1. Establish the purpose and evidence

Identify the reader, their prior knowledge, the question or task, and the main answer or learning
goal. Establish how the text will be used, such as a formal paper, a self-contained explanation,
or a spoken presentation. Match its register and explanatory detail to that use; choose sectioning
and heading forms separately. Infer these from context; ask only when a missing detail would
materially change the work.
Check that the available evidence supports the answer. Research essential gaps within the task's
scope, or make the uncertainty explicit.

Before planning the document, settle a brief statement of its purpose, the supported answer or
learning goal, and any material limits. For a revision, derive these from the existing document
and the requested change. Proceed when you can explain what the reader should understand,
evaluate, or do, and which evidence the document can rely on.

## 2. Plan the explanation

Plan the section or paragraph order and note what each part contributes. Introduce an essential
concept before relying on it, without overwhelming the opening with definitions. Include material
needed to understand, evaluate, or use the answer. Put optional detail where readers can consult
it without interrupting the explanation.

- For a report or recommendation, make the finding and its material limits clear near the start.
  For a tutorial, establish the goal and guide the reader through the task. Use reference sections
  for lookup. A document can combine these purposes when that serves its readers.
- Choose headings for the document's genre and hierarchy. Major sections in papers and technical
  documents usually name a subject or function, such as "Performance analysis" or "Evaluation".
  Put findings in the text, an optional subtitle, or an appropriate narrower subheading. Task or
  question headings can suit procedures and explanatory material. Use conclusion-style titles
  when they fit the purpose and presentation style, rather than as the default.

Read the planned sequence as a reader: does each part have a distinct purpose, and does the order
provide what the next part needs? Merge overlapping parts, move prerequisites, and remove material
that does not serve the document's purpose before drafting. Reuse an existing structure when it
already serves the reader.

## 3. Draft and revise the structure

Draft from the plan. Connect facts through their actual relationships, such as cause, consequence,
comparison, or evidence. Keep a condition with the claim it qualifies. Use concrete examples to
make an unfamiliar idea understandable. Use lists for parallel items and prose when it makes the
connections between ideas easier to follow.

### Use plain technical language

Write so the intended reader can understand the technical point without reconstructing omitted
relationships. Use only the explanation the reader needs. Remove wording that adds no meaning,
replace indirect expressions with direct ones, and add context or reasoning where it is missing.
A revision may be shorter, similar in length or longer; expansion is justified by useful content,
not by an assumption that more explanation is better.

Use ASD-STE100 as a guide to clarity, consistent terminology and direct sentences; this workflow
does not require its controlled vocabulary or establish formal compliance. Keep the standard
technical terms and mathematical notation needed for the subject.

For a substantial language revision, make brief notes of what the source asserts and how its
facts relate, separate from its prose. Keep exact technical names, quantities, conditions and
references, and identify missing information. Put the original sentences aside and draft from
those notes; then compare the draft with the source for technical meaning. Do not treat the
source's descriptive labels or sentence structure as facts that must survive. Review inherited
terminology as critically as terms you introduce. Preserve an author's named method while
explaining its meaning where necessary. For an independent rewrite, give the writer the checked
notes without the original prose, so the old sentences cannot serve as its template. Keep factual
notes out of the deliverable unless requested.

Make changes in subject or scope explicit. Establish the context needed to interpret a technical
term before using it, and connect that context to the preceding discussion. Assume the reader's
established technical knowledge, but do not assume they can infer an unstated change of topic.
Often a short qualifier is sufficient. Add definitions only when the intended reader needs them.

State proposals and decisions directly. Explain what will be investigated, what the alternatives
are, or what remains undecided. Do not make the reader infer those facts from a category or planning
label. Establish the main research question before compressing it into a summary name.

- **Choose terms by meaning and established usage.** Use the field's conventional name for an
  operation, quantity or component. Keep the same name for the same concept. If no conventional
  name is needed, use an ordinary phrase. Introduce a new term only when the concept needs a name,
  and explain it before relying on it. A compact label or a source's terminology is not automatically
  clearer than a description of what happens.
- **Explain the mechanism where the reader needs it.** Name the relevant component, what it does and
  how that affects the result. When several costs matter, explain their relationships. A list of
  resource names cannot establish why a design helps or fails. Explain an unfamiliar term at its
  first useful occurrence, using an example when the operation would otherwise remain abstract.
- **Make relationships explicit.** Expand a chain of nouns into a clause when it leaves the action
  or relationship unclear. Distinguish decisions made at different levels rather than joining
  them under an ambiguous heading. Preserve established noun phrases such as memory bandwidth;
  do not replace precise terminology merely because it is technical.
- **Use connected sentences.** Give each sentence a clear main statement and connect related
  statements through cause, condition or consequence. Split a sentence when its clauses compete
  for attention. Do not shorten it into fragments or force every sentence to the same length.
  Keep the explanation needed to follow the mechanism and remove wording that adds nothing.
- **Keep qualifications useful and local.** State the condition that changes a claim near that
  claim. Put supporting measurements and methodological detail where the reader needs them.
  Explain a limitation once clearly rather than repeating generic cautions throughout the text.
  Do not remove uncertainty, evidence limits or numerical conditions to make prose smoother.

Before accepting a paragraph, check whether the reader can identify the operation or decision,
understand the necessary terms and follow the claimed relationship. If the paragraph only names
categories, add the missing explanation or remove categories that do not help. Apply the same
check to headings, table cells, captions and summaries. Keep internal planning labels and task
history out of an explanation unless they help the reader make a decision.

Review the actual draft for structure before editing individual sentences:

1. Compare the opening with the final conclusions. It should deliver the main answer or establish
   the specific question the explanation resolves. Give a material blocker appropriate prominence.
2. Read the headings in sequence, then summarize each section's and paragraph's contribution in
   a short phrase. Use this to find repeated points, unexplained jumps, missing prerequisites, and
   material that does not serve the purpose. Move, cut, or add content to repair those problems.
3. Check that the evidence supports the claims and that shortening has preserved necessary
   reasoning and qualifications. A missing connection may require more explanation.

Re-read the affected passages after those changes. Complete this pass when the opening matches
the document's purpose, each part makes a needed contribution, and the explanation supplies the
connections and prerequisites the intended reader needs.

## 4. Review and verify the document

Finish the draft, then reread the actual saved or assembled text in a separate editorial step.
Editing while drafting or reading these instructions does not replace this second read. For a new
or extensively revised document, read the complete draft. For a focused revision, read enough
surrounding text to judge its meaning and continuity.

First check what the draft communicates on its own, before consulting source notes or an
explanation of the author's intent. For an unclear passage, identify its wording and the question
the intended reader needs answered but cannot answer from the text already presented. Check that
the subject needed to interpret its technical terms has been established. Repeating an unclear
label in a summary does not resolve the reader's question. Distinguish missing context
or reasoning from a familiar term that needs no definition. Leave adequate prose unchanged.

For an independent comprehension review, give the reviewer the intended reader and the draft
before providing sources or the author's explanation. Obtain the reader's questions first; keep
source verification separate.

Next check the facts and proposed technical corrections against the sources. A review finding can
be wrong; do not treat it as evidence. Before deleting a claim as unsupported, check the relevant
source passage. Preserve names, quantities, units, conditions and reference targets. Keep source
results distinct from interpretations and proposals. Do not strengthen a claim, change a
comparison or remove uncertainty. Keep modeled results distinct from measurements, and distinguish
implementation, testing and deployment status when it affects the claim.

Resolve the identified gaps and use `unslop` for the wording review. Keep terminology conventional
and consistent. Remove repetition, indirection, unnecessary definitions and task history that does
not help the reader. Add only missing context or reasoning; word count does not measure the
success of a revision.

Reread the changed passages in context to check for new ambiguity, disrupted flow or altered
meaning. Resolve the issues found before delivery. If the sources cannot resolve a material gap,
state what is missing. Do not claim a review from a word search or a completed checklist. Keep
routine review notes out of the document unless requested or required by the task.

For presented slides, make the topic or question clear and give the audience a takeaway supported
by evidence. Use topic, question, or message titles as appropriate to the deck. Review the slide
sequence to check that the audience can follow the explanation. A deck read independently needs
enough explanation to replace what a speaker would say. Select the evidence and context that fit
each slide; move supporting detail to notes or an appendix when needed. Qualify claims wherever
they appear.

Use available document, presentation, PDF, or LaTeX skills for format-specific production. Inspect
the rendered artifact when layout matters, and fix unreadable figures, crowded slides, awkward
page breaks, or headings separated from their content. Before delivery, check that the final
artifact communicates the intended message, supports its claims, and preserves material limits.
